Superimposition means playing the arpeggio of one chord over a different
chord. You already know the shapes — the trick is choosing which arpeggio to
play so its notes land on the extensions of the chord underneath. Em7 over
Cmaj7 gives you E, G, B, D: the **3, 5, 7, and 9** of C. Same fingering, a
richer sound.

## Stacking thirds

Every chord is a stack of thirds, and the stack doesn't have to stop at the
seventh. Keep going over Cmaj7 and you get the extensions:

| C | E | G | B | D | F♯ | A |
|---|---|---|---|---|----|---|
| R | 3 | 5 | 7 | 9 | ♯11 | 13 |

Any four neighbours in that stack form another seventh chord. Start on the 3rd
and you get **Em7** (3 5 7 9); start on the 5th and you get **Gmaj7**
(5 7 9 ♯11); start on the 7th and you get **Bm7** (7 9 ♯11 13). The higher you
start, the further you move from the root and the more colour you hear.

## The go-to choices

| Chord | Arpeggio | Notes over the chord | Sound |
|-------|----------|----------------------|-------|
| Cmaj7 | Em7 (iii) | 3 5 7 9 | Cmaj9 |
| Cmaj7 | Gmaj7 (V) | 5 7 9 ♯11 | Cmaj9(♯11), Lydian |
| Dm7 | Fmaj7 (♭III) | ♭3 5 ♭7 9 | Dm9 |
| Dm7 | Am7 (v) | 5 ♭7 9 11 | Dm11 |
| G7 | Bm7♭5 (iii) | 3 5 ♭7 9 | G9 |
| G7 | A♭°7 (♭ii) | ♭9 3 5 ♭7 | G7♭9 |
| G7 | D♭7 (♭V) | ♭5 ♭7 ♭9 3 | G7(♭9♯11), tritone sub |

The first row of each chord — the arpeggio **from the 3rd** — is the one to
learn first. It keeps the 3rd and 7th (the guide tones that define the chord),
drops the root the bass is already playing, and adds the 9. It almost never
sounds wrong.

## Why it works

- **The root is redundant.** In a band the bass covers it; leaving it out
  frees a note for colour.
- **The guide tones survive.** The lower superimpositions keep the 3rd and 7th,
  so the chord quality stays clear even as tensions appear.
- **The shapes are already in your hands.** You are not learning new
  fingerings, only new targets for the ones you know from
  [tetrad arpeggios](/lessons/arpeggios/tetrads).

## Over a ii–V–I

In C major, play the arpeggio from the 3rd of each chord:

| Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|-----|----|-------|
| Fmaj7 — F A C E | Bm7♭5 — B D F A | Em7 — E G B D |
| Dm9 | G9 | Cmaj9 |

Each arpeggio shares two notes with the next (F and A, then B and D), so the
line moves smoothly through the changes while every chord sounds as a ninth
chord.

## Over dominants: inside to outside

The dominant takes the widest range of superimpositions, because its tensions
can be natural or altered:

- **Bm7♭5 / Dm7 over G7** — inside, Mixolydian colour (9, 11).
- **A triad over G7** — 9 ♯11 13, the Lydian dominant sound.
- **A♭°7 over G7** — the ♭9, covered in
  [diminished arpeggios over dominants](/lessons/arpeggios/dim-over-dominants).
- **D♭7 over G7** — the tritone substitution; you hear ♭9 and ♯11.
- **E♭ triad over G7** — ♭13 and ♯9 around the root, an altered sound.

Save the altered options for dominants that resolve — V7 going to I — where
the tension has somewhere to go.

## Practice

1. **Chord, then superimposition.** Play the chord's own arpeggio, then the
   superimposed one in the same position. Hear the colour change.
2. **Over a drone.** Hold the root (or loop the chord) and play only the
   superimposed arpeggio. Listen for which notes ring as tensions.
3. **Resolve the top note.** End phrases on a guide tone — let the 9 fall to
   the root or the ♯11 to the 5.
4. **One per chord.** Take a tune like [Autumn Leaves](/songs/autumn-leaves)
   and play the arpeggio from the 3rd of every chord, then try the one from
   the 5th.
