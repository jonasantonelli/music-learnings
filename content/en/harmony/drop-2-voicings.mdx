Drop 2 voicings take a four-note chord stacked in close position and drop
the **second voice from the top** down an octave. The result spreads across
four adjacent strings with no big stretches, and it's the backbone of jazz
comping and chord melody on guitar.

Each chord has **four inversions**, named after the note in the bass, and each
inversion fits on **three string sets**. That's twelve shapes per chord
quality. Pick a quality, add a **color** to turn a seventh chord into a 9th,
11th or 13th, and switch to **Full neck** to see every position from the 1st
fret up to the 19th.

### How to read the diagrams

- Dot labels show the chord degree: **R**, **3** / **♭3**, **5** / **♭5** / **♯5**,
  **6**, **7** / **♭7** / **°7**, plus tensions like **9**, **♭9**, **11**, **♯11**, **13** or **♭13**
- The root is highlighted in the accent color
- Muted strings (×) are not part of the voicing
- Use **Notes** to see note names spelled by degree (C°7 is C–E♭–G♭–B𝄫, not
  C–E♭–G♭–A)

## What makes a voicing "drop 2"

### Start from a close voicing

A **close voicing** packs all four notes inside one octave, stacked in thirds.
On piano it falls straight under the hand. On guitar it usually needs an
awkward stretch, because adjacent strings are a 4th apart and the notes of a
close voicing are only a 3rd apart.

### Drop the second voice from the top

Take **Cmaj7** in close position, from the top down: **B – G – E – C**.
The second voice from the top is **G**. Move it down an octave and it becomes
the new bass:

| Voice      | Close position | Drop 2      |
| ---------- | -------------- | ----------- |
| Top        | B              | B           |
| 2nd        | G              | — (dropped) |
| 3rd        | E              | E           |
| 4th        | C              | C           |
| New bottom | —              | G           |

From the bottom up, the drop 2 voicing reads **G – C – E – B**: a 5th in the
bass, then the root, the 3rd and the 7th. The voicing now spans a 10th
instead of a 7th. That wider spacing matches the guitar's tuning, so it falls
onto four adjacent strings.

### Four inversions

Apply the same drop to each of the four close inversions and you get four
drop 2 inversions. On guitar they're named after the **bass note**:

| Inversion     | Bass | Low → high  | Comes from close |
| ------------- | ---- | ----------- | ---------------- |
| Root position | R    | R – 5 – 7 – 3 | 5 – 7 – R – 3  |
| 1st inversion | 3    | 3 – 7 – R – 5 | 7 – R – 3 – 5  |
| 2nd inversion | 5    | 5 – R – 3 – 7 | R – 3 – 5 – 7  |
| 3rd inversion | 7    | 7 – 3 – 5 – R | 3 – 5 – 7 – R  |

Notice the voice order cycles: **R 5 7 3 → 3 7 R 5 → 5 R 3 7 → 7 3 5 R**.
Every chord tone moves up to the next one. That's why the four shapes sit
next to each other on the neck and repeat every 12 frets.

### Three string sets

| String set | Character                                                    |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 6-5-4-3    | Dark and heavy; good for big-band comping and low pads        |
| 5-4-3-2    | The workhorse; full but clear, and it sits in the mid-range   |
| 4-3-2-1    | Bright and light; ideal for chord melody, where the top note is the melody |

The same inversion looks slightly different on each set, and the reason is
always the B string. Every other pair of adjacent strings is a 4th apart, but
G–B is a major 3rd. So when you move a shape up a string set, any note that
crosses onto the B (or high E) string moves **up one fret**. Learn that one
adjustment and you can move any shape between string sets.

## The chord qualities

| Family     | Quality | Formula        | Typical use                                     |
| ---------- | ------- | -------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Major      | maj7    | R 3 5 7        | I and IV in major keys                           |
|            | 6       | R 3 5 6        | Tonic major; swing-era sound, no clash with a melody root |
|            | maj7♯5  | R 3 ♯5 7       | ♭III in melodic and harmonic minor; Lydian augmented |
| Minor      | m7      | R ♭3 5 ♭7      | ii, iii and vi; Dorian / Aeolian                   |
|            | m6      | R ♭3 5 6       | Minor tonic (melodic minor); also a rootless V9   |
|            | m(maj7) | R ♭3 5 7       | Minor tonic; the "James Bond" chord               |
| Dominant   | 7       | R 3 5 ♭7       | V; blues; secondary dominants                      |
|            | 7sus4   | R 4 5 ♭7       | Suspended V; modal tunes (Maiden Voyage)           |
|            | 7♯5     | R 3 ♯5 ♭7      | Altered/whole-tone V resolving to I                |
|            | 7♭5     | R 3 ♭5 ♭7      | Tritone-sub flavor; French augmented 6th           |
| Diminished | m7♭5    | R ♭3 ♭5 ♭7     | iiø in minor ii–V; also vii in major              |
|            | dim7    | R ♭3 ♭5 °7     | Passing chord; rootless V7♭9                       |

**Shared shapes:** **C6** and **Am7** contain the same four notes, so every C6
shape is also an Am7 shape. Likewise **Cm6** is **Am7♭5**. One shape, two
names; learn the pair together. **dim7** is symmetrical: each inversion is
the same shape moved up 3 frets.

## Colors: extending drop 2 chords

A drop 2 voicing has only four notes, so adding a 9th or 13th means
**replacing** a chord tone rather than piling on another. The rule jazz
guitarists use:

- **The root, 3rd and 7th stay.** The 3rd and 7th are the guide tones that
  define the chord; the root anchors the sound.
- **The 5th becomes the tension** (9, ♭9, ♯9, 11, ♯11, 13 or ♭13). The 5th
  adds the least color, so it's the first to go.

Four voices fit only one tension alongside the root. So here m11 means
R ♭3 ♭7 11, and maj13 / 13 mean R 3 7 13: the 9 is left out. For example,
Dm9 on 5-4-3-2 is x-5-3-5-5-x (R ♭3 ♭7 9), and Dm11 is x-5-5-5-6-x
(R 11 ♭7 ♭3).

The 11 and the 13s sit a step from the 5th, so for those **the shape barely
changes**: move one finger up a whole step and a maj7 becomes a maj13. The 9s
sit between the root and the 3rd, so the 9 shapes change a little more.

| Base    | 5 → 9 / ♭9 / ♯9  | 5 → 11 / ♯11 / 13 / ♭13   |
| ------- | ---------------- | ------------------------- |
| maj7    | maj9             | maj13, maj7(♯11)          |
| 6       | 6/9              | —                         |
| m7      | m9               | m11                       |
| m6      | m6/9             | —                         |
| m(maj7) | m(maj9)          | —                         |
| 7       | 9, 7♭9, 7♯9      | 13, 7♭13, 7♯11            |
| 7sus4   | 9sus4, 7sus4(♭9) | 13sus4                    |

**Exception: altered 5ths.** In maj7♯5, 7♯5, 7♭5 and m7♭5 the altered 5th
defines the chord and can't go. So maj9♯5, 9♯5, 7♯5♭9, 9♭5, 7♭5♭9 and m9♭5
are **rootless voicings**: the 9 takes the root's place. That's common in
ensemble playing, since the bassist already plays the root. Solo, play the
root on a lower string first, or let the context imply it.

**Choosing a dominant color.** Dominants resolving to **major** usually take
natural tensions (9, 13). Dominants resolving to **minor** take altered ones
(♭9, ♭13). Dominants that don't resolve (blues, a static vamp) take 9, 13 or
♯11. The ii–V page has an **Extended** mode that applies these choices
automatically.

## Practice plan

1. **One quality, one string set.** Learn the four maj7 inversions on 5-4-3-2,
   saying the bass degree aloud as you play each one.
2. **Climb the neck.** Play root position → 1st → 2nd → 3rd → root position
   (octave). Use **Full neck** to see where the cycle repeats.
3. **Shift string sets.** Play the same inversion on all three sets. Watch the
   B-string adjustment on 4-3-2-1.
4. **Add colors one finger at a time.** maj7 → maj13 (5th up a whole step),
   then 7 → 7♭13 (5th up a half step), then m7 → m11 (5th down a whole step).
   Feel which finger moves.
5. **Use them in context.** Take the shapes to the
   [ii–V–I voice leading](/lessons/harmony/drop-2-ii-v-i) page, then to
   [diatonic harmony](/lessons/harmony/drop-2-diatonic) to run every chord of
   a key.
