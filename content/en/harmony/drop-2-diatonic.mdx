Stack thirds on every note of a scale and you get its seven **diatonic
seventh chords**: the full chord vocabulary of a key. Playing them in drop 2,
**one inversion at a time**, is the classic way to connect the shapes from
the [drop 2 voicings](/lessons/harmony/drop-2-voicings) page into a map of the
neck.

Pick a key, a scale and a **bass note** (which inversion to use), then play
the eight chords in order. Every voice moves up to the next note of the
scale, so the shape walks up the neck one scale step at a time.

If a run reaches the top of the neck before it finishes, the remaining chords
continue an octave lower, marked **8va↓**.

## The three harmonized scales

| Degree | Major      | Melodic minor | Harmonic minor |
| ------ | ---------- | ------------- | -------------- |
| 1      | Imaj7      | i–(maj7)      | i–(maj7)       |
| 2      | ii–7       | ii–7          | iiø7           |
| 3      | iii–7      | ♭IIImaj7♯5    | ♭IIImaj7♯5     |
| 4      | IVmaj7     | IV7           | iv–7           |
| 5      | V7         | V7            | V7             |
| 6      | vi–7       | viø7          | ♭VImaj7        |
| 7      | viiø7      | viiø7         | vii°7          |

### Major

The source of nearly every progression in standard repertoire: ii–V–I,
I–vi–ii–V and iii–vi–ii–V are all drawn from this column. Only **one**
dominant chord (V7) appears, which is why it has such a strong pull home.

### Melodic minor

The jazz minor scale (ascending form) is a minor scale with a major 6th and 7th.
It's the go-to source for **minor tonic** chords (m(maj7), m6) and for
"outside" dominant sounds. It has **two** dominants: **IV7** is the Lydian ♭7
sound (♯11), and **V7** gives a ♭13 sound. The two half-diminished chords on 6
and 7 are where the altered and locrian ♮2 sounds come from.

### Harmonic minor

A natural minor scale with a raised 7th. That raised 7th creates the **V7**
that makes minor ii–V–i resolve, the **iiø7** that precedes it, and a
**vii°7**: four symmetrical notes that also work as a rootless V7♭9. The
♭IIImaj7♯5 is the same chord as in melodic minor.

## How to practice

1. **One inversion, one string set.** Start with root position on 5-4-3-2 in
   C major. Say each chord name aloud as you play it.
2. **Up and back down.** Play the run ascending, then descending. Going down
   is harder, and that's where the learning happens.
3. **All four bass notes.** Repeat with the 3rd, 5th and 7th in the bass.
   Each inversion starts in a different part of the neck. Together they give
   you all 28 shapes of the key on that string set (7 chords × 4 inversions).
4. **Move one string set.** On 4-3-2-1 the top voice is the melody: play the
   run and listen to the scale on top.
5. **Skip around.** Jump by 4ths instead of steps (I → IV → vii → iii → vi
   → ii → V → I). That's the diatonic cycle, the skeleton of
   [Autumn Leaves](/songs/autumn-leaves).
6. **Compare the minors.** Play the same inversion in C melodic minor and C
   harmonic minor back to back. Hear which chords change and which stay.
