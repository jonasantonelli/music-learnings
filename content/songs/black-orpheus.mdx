## Study Notes

Luiz Bonfá wrote *Manhã de Carnaval* for the 1959 film *Orfeu Negro*, and it became the tune that carried bossa nova into the American jazz repertoire. The version to study here is **Paul Desmond's "The Theme from 'Black Orpheus'"** from *Take Ten* (RCA Victor, 1963) — Desmond on alto, **Jim Hall** on guitar, Gene Cherico on bass, Connie Kay on drums, recorded in June 1963.

The chart above is in **G minor**, the key of the recording — a whole step below the A minor most fake books print, so transpose if you're reading along from a lead sheet.

What makes this version worth transcribing isn't harmonic complexity — the changes are the standard ones. It's the **restraint**. There's no piano, so Hall's guitar leaves most of the harmony implied, and Desmond plays a melody that is already lyrical without adding a single ornament to it. If you tend to overplay bossas, this recording is the corrective.

### The key relationship — G minor and Bb major

The whole tune is one idea repeated: **a minor ii-V-i in G minor, answered by a major ii-V-I in Bb major**. Everything else is decoration.

- `Am7b5 → D7(b9) → Gm` is the minor cadence (bars 2–3, 4–5, 14–15, and again at the end of each half).
- `Cm7 → F7 → BbMaj7` is the major cadence (bars 6–7, 9–11).

G minor and Bb major are relatives — the same seven notes — so you can play G natural minor over most of the tune and never hit a wrong note. That's exactly the trap. The tune only comes alive if you **hear which of the two tonics you're pointed at** and land on the right note when you get there: Bb/D/F at bar 7 and bar 11, G/Bb/D at bar 3 and bar 15.

**Practice idea:** play the head using only quarter notes on chord tones, and mark every arrival bar with a long note. You'll feel the tune tilting back and forth between the two keys.

### A section, bar by bar

**Bars 1–4 (Gm6 · Am7b5 D7(b9) · Gm6 · Am7b5 D7(b9)):** The signature rocking figure. The tonic is written `Gm6` rather than `Gm7` on purpose — the E♮ in the 6th is what gives bossa minor its bright, non-mournful color. **G Dorian** (or G melodic minor) fits the `Gm6`; over `Am7b5` use **A Locrian ♮2** (from Bb melodic minor), and over `D7(b9)` use **D Phrygian dominant** or the **half-whole diminished** scale. The b9 (Eb) and b13 (Bb) both belong to G minor, so the alteration isn't an outside color — it's simply the key.

**Bars 5–7 (Gm7 · Cm7 F7 · BbMaj7):** The pivot to the relative major. `Gm7` is now functioning as vi of Bb, not as i of G minor — same chord, different job. Harmonic rhythm doubles in bar 6, so keep the line moving. **Bb major** works across all three bars; if you want more shape, use **C Dorian** on `Cm7` and **F Mixolydian** on `F7`.

**Bar 8 (G7(b9)):** The first genuinely chromatic bar — G7 is **V/ii**, a secondary dominant aiming at the `Cm7` in bar 9. The B♮ turns the tonic minor chord into a dominant, which is the strongest surprise in the tune. Play **G Phrygian dominant** or **G half-whole diminished**, and make sure the B♮ is audible. A very common comping substitution here is **Bdim7** (B–D–F–Ab), which is `G7(b9)` without its root — Hall's kind of chord, since it puts the tension in the middle voices.

**Bars 9–12 (Cm7 · F7 · BbMaj7 · EbMaj7):** The full ii-V-I in Bb, then a step up to `EbMaj7` (IV of Bb, bVI of G minor). The EbMaj7 is a soft landing, not a cadence — **Eb Lydian** is the natural choice, and the A♮ in Eb Lydian is also the leading tone of the `Am7b5` coming next, so the scale sets up bar 13 for free.

**Bars 13–16 (Am7b5 · D7(b9) · Gm6 · Am7b5 D7(b9)):** Back to the minor cadence, now stretched over two bars so it feels like a real arrival. Bar 16 restarts the loop.

### B section — the C minor detour

The second half opens as a copy of the first, then breaks away.

**Bars 21–24 (Dm7b5 · G7(b9) · Cm7 · Cm7):** A **minor ii-V-i into C minor**, the iv of the home key. This is the emotional low point of the tune and the one place where G minor no longer covers you: the **Ab** in `Dm7b5` and the **B♮** in `G7(b9)` are both foreign to the key. Use **D Locrian ♮2**, then **G Phrygian dominant**, then **C Dorian** (Bonfá's melody keeps the A♮, so Dorian, not Aeolian).

**Bars 25–28 (Cm7 Cm7/Bb · Am7b5 D7(b9) · Gm7 Gm7/F · EbMaj7):** A **descending bass line** — C → Bb → A → D → G → F → Eb. The slash chords aren't new harmony, they're a bass walk, so don't re-voice your lines for them; just let the bass fall. This is the passage where Hall's comping matters most: he lets the top voices sit still while the bottom moves.

**Bars 29–32 (Am7b5 · D7(b9) · Gm6 · Am7b5 D7(b9)):** Final minor cadence, then the turnaround back to the top.

### Playing it Desmond's way

- **State the melody straight.** Bonfá wrote long, stepwise phrases with built-in space. Desmond adds almost nothing to them on the head — his ornament is the *tone*, not the notes.
- **Play behind the beat, not on it.** Bossa is not swung. The eighths are even; the phrasing floats by placing phrase entrances late and letting them drift back.
- **Develop cells, don't run changes.** Desmond's solos are motivic — a three- or four-note shape stated, transposed, and reshaped across the harmony. Try improvising a chorus using one four-note motif only, moving it through the G-minor/Bb-major alternation.
- **Leave the tonic alone.** Because G minor and Bb major share a scale, resolving to G too often makes the tune flat. Aim for the 3rds and 6ths — Bb and E♮ over `Gm6`, D and G over `BbMaj7`.
- **Alto players:** concert G minor is **E minor** on an Eb horn — an easy, resonant key on the instrument, and probably part of why this tempo and register sit so comfortably for Desmond.
- **Guitarists:** Jim Hall's role here is a masterclass in comping without a piano. Voice in three notes, keep the 3rd and 7th, and let the bass supply the roots. The `Cm7/Bb` and `Gm7/F` bars are where the whole approach becomes audible.
