Connect drop 2 voicings through the progressions that make up most of the
jazz repertoire. Each row starts from a different inversion of the first
chord; every chord after it is the **nearest voicing on the same string
set**, so the hand barely moves.

Switch **Chords** to **Extended** to hear the same voice leading with the
colors a working guitarist would use: m9, 13 and maj9 in major, and 7♭9♭13 in
minor.

## Guide tones: why ii–V–I voice-leads so well

The 3rd and 7th of each chord, its **guide tones**, define its quality. In a
ii–V–I they move by the smallest possible steps:

| Change         | 7th of first chord        | 3rd of first chord       |
| -------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Dm7 → G7       | C ↓ half step → B (3 of G7) | F stays → F (♭7 of G7)  |
| G7 → Cmaj7     | F ↓ half step → E (3 of C)  | B stays → B (7 of Cmaj7) |

So the 7th resolves down a half step to the next 3rd, and the 3rd holds over
to become the next 7th. Meanwhile the root and 5th move by step too. In a
major ii–V–I, each change moves the four voices a **total of three
semitones**.

### The inversion pattern

Look at the subtitles under the diagrams: **the ii and the I land in the same
inversion, and the V sits two inversions away**.

| Start (ii)    | V             | I             |
| ------------- | ------------- | ------------- |
| Root position | 2nd inversion | Root position |
| 1st inversion | 3rd inversion | 1st inversion |
| 2nd inversion | Root position | 2nd inversion |
| 3rd inversion | 1st inversion | 3rd inversion |

Memorize this table and you can find a smooth ii–V–I from any shape, in any
key, without thinking. (Near the top or bottom of the neck a row may jump an
octave because the ideal shape runs out of frets.)

### Minor ii–V–i

In minor the ii is half-diminished (**m7♭5**) and the V usually takes
**altered** tensions: the ♭9 and ♭13 are notes from the minor key itself.
For the tonic, choose:

- **m7**: the Dorian / natural-minor sound (Autumn Leaves, Blue Bossa)
- **m6**: brighter, melodic-minor; the classic bossa and swing tonic
- **m(maj7)**: darker and more dramatic; often a passing color before m6

## Turnarounds

A **turnaround** cycles back to the top of a form. All three below are built
from ii–V motion, so the same inversion pattern applies link by link.

- **I–vi–ii–V**: the "rhythm changes" A section and countless song endings.
- **I–VI7–ii–V**: the vi becomes a **secondary dominant** (V7/ii). In
  extended mode it takes a ♭9 because it resolves to a minor chord.
- **iii–VI7–ii–V–I**: replaces the I with its substitute iii, giving a chain
  of resolutions down in 5ths.

The A section of [Have You Met Miss Jones](/songs/have-you-met-miss-jones)
opens with **Fmaj7 – F♯°7 – Gm7 – C7 | Am7 – Dm7 – Gm7 – C7**: a
I–VI7–ii–V where the VI7 appears as **F♯°7**, followed by a iii–vi–ii–V. F♯°7
is D7♭9 without its root, so try the **I–VI7–ii–V** row in F with
**Extended** on: the D7♭9 shapes there (♭9 in place of the root) are
exactly the F♯°7 shapes.

## How to practice

1. **Slow, sustained, legato.** Let each chord ring and listen to the one or
   two voices that move. If you can sing the top voice, you're hearing it.
2. **Find the anchors.** Notice which notes stay on the same fret between
   chords. Keep those fingers down.
3. **All four rows, one string set.** Each row covers a different area of
   the neck. Together they map the fretboard for this key.
4. **Basic, then Extended.** Play a row with seventh chords, then switch to
   Extended. The shapes change by one or two fingers, not a whole new grip.
5. **Around the cycle.** Move the key around the circle of 4ths (C, F, B♭,
   E♭…), staying in the same area of the neck. This is what you'll do on a
   real tune.
6. **Apply it.** Comp through a [song chart](/songs) using only the voicings
   from one row per ii–V.
